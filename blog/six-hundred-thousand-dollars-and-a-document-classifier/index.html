Consultants wanted $600,000 to sort our files

Nearly a million document records, a move from Workday to Banner, and a quote with a lot of zeros. We passed, and I built the classifier myself.

12 Sep, 2026 · updated 19 Oct, 2026

Nine hundred forty-six thousand and ninety-one. That’s how many document records we had to move from Workday into Banner, every one of them into the right folder, under the right document type, attached to the right person. More than half a million actual files: financial aid forms, admissions paperwork, invoices, W-9s, expense reports. Years of the stuff.

When the president of Indian River State College decided we were moving to Banner, it sounded like a fresh start. New system, clean slate! Except nothing about a migration is ever clean, because you have to drag the past along with you. And our past was filed in categories so broad they said nothing about where anything belonged on the other side.

This is exactly the kind of problem that makes consultants’ eyes light up. It’s huge, it’s scary, it smells like risk, and so naturally the price tag follows suit. One outside company put the classification work alone at $600,000. Six hundred thousand dollars! To sort files!

We passed. I built the classifier in-house instead, and that $600k is the spending we avoided. (That’s where the savings number comes from, before anyone asks.)

Here’s what scary-problem pricing never accounts for: most of the evidence was already sitting right there in the export. Filenames, paths, the old categories. You don’t need a team of analysts to read a filename. You need rules a person can actually read, and an output the new system will actually accept.

So that’s what I built. Python rules around approved phrases and destinations. Every document type came from a sheet signed off by the people who own those records, and I didn’t invent a single mapping. And, crucially, known names and IDs get masked before any matching happens, including names that turn up elsewhere in the input.

Why does that matter so much? Because my favorite bug in the whole project was two letters long. DE can mean Dual Enrollment. It can also be the “De” in somebody’s name. Let a matcher get a little too enthusiastic, and a student’s name becomes evidence about what kind of document they submitted. Tiny bug. Now repeat it across nearly a million records! So the rule is simple: a filename has to say something about the document itself before it earns a placement.

When the evidence conflicted, the record went to an approved Legacy folder. Yes, that makes the percentage look worse. I don’t care! 606,330 records landed on a specific document type, and the other 339,761 went to Legacy instead of getting a guess. I could have juiced that 64% into something prettier. But the person digging through the wrong folder three years from now isn’t going to be consoled by my dashboard.

Now, there is a model-assisted path. It can use a local model through Ollama, or a cloud backend if someone explicitly flips it on. Either way, the model only ever sees redacted input, and its suggestions sit and wait for a human to approve them. But the rules didn’t need it. The documented sample run, 1,887 records, made zero LLM calls. Zero!

Then there was the file we actually had to hand over. Workday’s exports turned out to be .txt files with CSV inside and property values that looked almost, but not quite, like JSON. Lovely! And getting the categories right was worth precisely nothing if the corrected index choked the importer. So the writer keeps the source formatting and swaps only the document-type value, with tests for line endings, quoting, reordered properties, and mismatched headers. Every reviewer change gets a stable record hash and lands in an audit trail.

And Banner had opinions. No apostrophes in first or last names. Filenames under 100 characters, so we capped them at 99. HEIC photos had to become JPGs. And one CSV with nearly a million rows was more than the load wanted to swallow, so we split it into 38 files of at most 25,000 rows. Same header, same order, same rules in every chunk.

Then two of those 38 files bounced. 3,322 records had no folder, and every one of them was named something like Review Document_SF_FinAid_Authorization.pdf. That’s Workday’s internal form code for a book authorization. The other 96,510 book authorizations said “book authorization” in plain English, so the rules caught them. These 3,322 only spoke in form codes. One new rule, two patched files, back in the queue.

Before any of it shipped, the output was checked against the source: 946,092 lines counting the header, zero unknown document types, zero changes anywhere changes weren’t allowed, and 694,173 Banner IDs carried through intact.

That’s the whole trick, really. Get close enough to an intimidating problem and it stops being intimidating. It turns into a list: a filing rule, somebody’s name, a form code, the exact shape of a text file, a 25,000-row limit. Not one item on that list needed a $600,000 proposal.

So the next time someone hands you a scary quote for a scary problem, open the export first. Read some filenames. You might just find you can build the thing yourself.
