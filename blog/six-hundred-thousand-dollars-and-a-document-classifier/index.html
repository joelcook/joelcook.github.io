The $600k proposal and the filing problem

Years of documents, inconsistent filing, and a move from Workday to Banner. I built the classifier in-house.

12 Sep, 2026

The president of Indian River State College decided the school was moving from Workday to Banner. A new system, a fresh start.

Except we had years of documents to bring with us.

The filing habits hadn’t kept up with the volume. Documents had accumulated in broad categories that didn’t tell us where they belonged in Banner. Changing systems meant dealing with all that history.

One outside company’s proposal put the work at $600,000.

I built classification software to prepare the document indexes in-house. That $600k is the competing proposal we avoided, which is the basis for the savings figure. The work was to turn an enormous filing problem into rules we could inspect and an output the new system could use.

One of the more interesting problems was two letters long.

DE could mean Dual Enrollment. It could also appear as “De” in somebody’s name. A classifier that was a little too enthusiastic about matching text could take a student’s name as evidence of what kind of document they had submitted.

That is a very small bug to repeat across a very large migration.

The useful evidence was already in the export: filenames, paths, and existing categories. I built Python rules around approved phrases and destinations, normalized the separators, and masked known names and IDs before matching. The masking included names found elsewhere in the input corpus. A filename needed to say something about the document itself before it could justify a placement.

When the evidence conflicted, the pipeline used an approved fallback. There is a temptation to keep increasing the number of specific classifications because a higher percentage looks better. But an impressive percentage is little comfort to the person looking in the wrong folder later.

The documented sample run covered 1,887 index rows. Of those, 1,396 received specific document types and 491 went to an approved Legacy Document destination. That was a sample of the migration, not a claim that every document had been inspected or that classification accuracy was 100%. The run made no LLM calls.

I added a model-assisted path too. It can use a local model through Ollama or an explicitly enabled cloud backend. Both receive redacted inputs, and their suggestions wait for a human’s approval. The rules can do their work without waiting for that machinery.

Then there was the file we actually had to hand over.

Workday’s exports had a .txt extension, CSV contents, and property values that looked almost like JSON. The destination needed a corrected index. Getting the categories right was only useful if that index survived the importer.

So the writer preserves the source formatting while replacing the document-type value. The parser finds that property by its key, even if the columns move. Tests cover line endings, quoting, reordered properties, and differing headers in the merged output. Reviewer changes have stable record hashes and an audit trail.

I enjoy this part of engineering: getting close enough to a problem that its intimidating size gives way to specific things you can fix. In this case, those things included a filing rule, a person’s name, and the exact shape of a text file.

The decision to leave Workday was made at the top. Making those years of documents usable in Banner came down to the details in the export. I’m glad we looked closely enough to build the tool ourselves.
