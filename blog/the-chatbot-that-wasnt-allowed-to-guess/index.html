Apparently, students do want a chatbot

$47k a year cheaper. More than 9,000 chats a week. And it all started with a bot that made students guess the magic words.

23 Jun, 2026 · updated 6 Oct, 2026

The college already had a chatbot. It worked great, as long as you asked your question exactly the way it expected. Use different words for something it knew perfectly well, and you’d get nowhere. It was 2026! Surely we could do better than making students guess how we worded our FAQs.

I’ll confess that I thought people hated chatbots. The numbers have humbled me: the replacement now handles more than 9,000 chats a week. Nine thousand! Turns out plenty of people are perfectly happy to open a chat, ask a question, and get on with their day.

And why wouldn’t they be? A student trying to register for a class has a job to do. They shouldn’t have to learn which department owns which page, or how we decided to organize the website. Just give them the freaking answer!

I built the replacement at Indian River State College, and it saves $47,000 a year. Pretty sweet. But the cheaper bill was the easy part. The hard part, the part that separates an institutional assistant from a toy chat box, is deciding where the answers come from.

Credit where it’s due: Andrej Karpathy’s LLM wiki gist lays out the idea of compiling source material into a persistent, interlinked wiki that an agent can maintain and consult. That was my starting point for the knowledge layer, and it’s a wonderful idea.

But a college needs one more distinction. Explanations can live in the wiki, linked back to their sources. Facts that change, like tuition and drop deadlines, belong in SQLite, and the assistant has to look them up. Every single time. No winging it.

Why so strict? Because a plausible answer is easy. Any model will happily hand you a confident-sounding date. A useful answer needs the right date for the right term, from the right source. And when an answer is wrong, I want somewhere specific to look: the source, the database projection, the retrieved page, the tool call. “Tell the model to be more careful” is not a debugging strategy!

Under the hood, it’s 12 read-only MCP tools, served from Azure Container Apps and wired into Copilot Studio. Official sources feed both the database and the wiki. Refreshing sources, rewriting explanations, and deploying the app are all separate operations.

Now here’s the part that really opened my eyes. In one Copilot Studio review, the general-quality judge marked correct privacy refusals as incomplete. The assistant had declined to hand out private student information, which is precisely what it should do, and the judge wanted a more complete answer. Think about that for a second. The grader docked the bot for doing the right thing!

So read your failures before you celebrate a score. Or panic over one.

The eval set now has 53 cases, covering answers, required tool calls, conflicting sources, and refusals. And if the model gets the right tuition figure without making the required lookup? The case fails anyway. I don’t want lucky answers. I want behavior that survives the next tuition change.

There’s an optional LLM judge for narrative answers, but it only runs after the deterministic checks. The local tools gate runs without paid inference, and checking the production model is its own separate job. Keep those results apart, and they’re actually worth reading.

Now, I can’t promise this thing will never make something up. Nobody can, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. But I can give it better sources, test how it behaves, and chase down failures with evidence. At 9,000 chats a week, that’s worth a whole lot more than another slick demo.

Students get to ask in their own words, and the college pays $47k a year less for the privilege. Open the chat, ask the question, get a real answer. That’s all anyone ever wanted!
