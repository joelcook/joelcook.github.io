Apparently, students do want a chatbot

$47k saved per year. More than 9,000 weekly chats. It started with a bot that needed the question worded just right.

23 Jun, 2026 · updated 12 Sep, 2026

The college already had a chatbot. To get a useful answer, you had to phrase the question the way it expected. A student could ask about something it knew and still get nowhere because they used different words.

It was 2026. Surely we could do better than making people guess the wording of our FAQs.

I also thought people hated chatbots. The usage gave me a reason to reconsider: the replacement now handles more than 9,000 chats a week. There is clearly an audience for opening a chat, asking a question, and getting on with your day.

I understand the appeal. A student trying to register for a class has a task to finish. They shouldn’t need to learn which department owns a page or how we organized the website. Just give them the freaking answer.

I built the replacement at Indian River State College, saving $47,000 a year. Then came the part that makes an institutional assistant harder than a chat box: deciding where its answers should come from.

Andrej Karpathy deserves a thank-you here. His LLM wiki gist describes compiling source material into a persistent, interlinked wiki that an agent can maintain and consult. I used that idea as the starting point for the knowledge layer.

The school’s information needed one extra distinction. Explanations could live in the wiki, with links back to their sources. Changing facts belonged in SQLite. A question about tuition or a drop deadline should trigger a lookup of the relevant record.

The model could explain the process. It also needed a reliable way to find which date applied to which term. A plausible answer was going to be easy; a useful one needed the right source.

That means I have somewhere specific to look when an answer is wrong. I can inspect the source, the database projection, the retrieved page, and the tool call. Telling the model to be more careful gives me considerably less to work with.

The interface is 12 read-only MCP tools served through Azure Container Apps and connected to Copilot Studio. Official sources feed the database and wiki. Refreshing the sources, rewriting explanations, and deploying the serving app remain separate operations.

But the most revealing part of the work came from evaluating the answers.

In one Copilot Studio review, the general-quality judge marked correct privacy refusals as incomplete. The assistant had declined to provide private student information. The judge wanted a more complete answer.

That is a good reason to read the failures before celebrating or despairing over a score.

The evaluation set now has 53 cases covering answers, required tool calls, conflicting sources, and refusals. If the model supplies the right tuition figure without making the required lookup, the case fails. I want the behavior to survive the next tuition change.

There is an optional LLM judge for narrative answers, after deterministic checks. The local tools gate runs without paid inference; checking the production model is a separate job. Keeping those results distinct makes them useful.

I can’t promise that the assistant will never invent an answer. I can give it better sources, test its behavior, and investigate failures with evidence. At 9,000 chats a week, that work matters more to me than another impressive demo.

The replacement costs $47k less per year, and students can ask questions in their own words. That is the experience I wanted: open the chat, ask the question, get a useful answer.
